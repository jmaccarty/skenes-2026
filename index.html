Skenes' Fall, Some Hypotheses Backed by Data

A year ago Paul Skenes was not just the best young pitcher in baseball, but the best pitcher period. There was discussion of him becoming an all time great on most talk radio shows in Pittsburgh. 2026, however, has been a different story, so I went through all 8,000+ pitches of his career. I looked for physical differences by pitch (velo, spin, etc.) trying to find a story and then test that story with the data.

TL;DR

The hypotheses this analysis tests

Skenes and the Pirates could see the fastball slowing. He came back in 2026 throwing from a higher arm slot, which tightened the tunnel between his pitches and made them harder to tell apart out of the hand, but at the cost of spin and location.

Skenes is having a bad season by his own standards, so the obvious question is what changed, but the more useful question turned out to be when. A pitcher who was bad all year and a pitcher who was excellent for nine starts and then stopped being excellent produce almost the same season line, and they are not the same problem.

  • He is a long way off last year. About 11 runs saved against roughly 42 in 2025, walk rate 5.7% to 7.1%, and 15 home runs already in fewer plate appearances than the 11 he allowed across all of last season.
  • But he opened the year better than his 2025 self. A .218 xwOBA and 138 consecutive batters without a walk. Every bit of his positive value was banked by 12 May, in nine starts.
  • Blind to every result, the physics found the break. Four unsupervised changepoint methods run on release-and-movement data alone land on 9–20 June. A separate detector that sees only outcomes lands on 20 June by itself.
  • There are two events, not one, and they broke different things. Over the winter his arm angle rose 1.8–3.5° on all five pitches and the fastball lost 1.33 mph. From mid-June spin collapsed 107–151 rpm across the arsenal while the league gained 25.
  • His arsenal compressed toward a slower fastball. The changeup has shed 3.4 mph of separation since 2024, and each dead pitch died in the matchup it was built for: the changeup against lefties, the sinker against righties.
  • None of this identifies a cause. He made 28 starts on normal rest with no IL stint (i.e. statcast cannot separate a redesign from an arm issue from fatigue).

Skenes is a long way off last year. He's saved about 11 runs in 2026 against roughly 42 in 2025, his walk rate is up from 5.7% to 7.1%, and he's already allowed 15 home runs in fewer plate appearances than the 11 he gave up across all of last season.

But he did not start the year badly, which is the part that makes this interesting. Through nine starts he was pitching better than his 2025 self: a .218 xwOBA, a 3.8% walk rate, and a run of 138 consecutive batters faced without walking anybody from April 13 to May 17, covering five straight starts without a single free pass. Every bit of positive value he has this season was banked in those nine outings. He topped out at +16.8 runs saved on May 12 and has been giving it back ever since.

So I pulled all 8,140 pitches he has thrown since his debut and asked a narrow question: is anything physically different about the baseballs leaving his hand? Arm angle, spin rate, velocity, spin axis, vertical and horizontal break, extension, release height and release side, each computed separately for all five of his pitches and adjusted for count, batter handedness, park, times through the order and days of rest. Then I let unsupervised changepoint methods hunt for a regime shift without telling them when to look.

Two things came out of it rather than one. They happened five months apart and they broke different parts of the delivery.

Change one, over the winter

He came back throwing from a higher slot

This is the cleanest signal in the dataset. His arm angle rose on all five pitches at once, between 1.8° and 3.5°, and 2024 and 2025 sit right on top of each other before it. Five pitch types moving the same direction in the same offseason is one delivery change showing up everywhere, not five separate tweaks. Everything downstream follows from it: the fastball lost 1.33 mph (the single largest shift in the whole dataset), its spin axis rotated 5°, and it picked up 1.3″ more ride while shedding 1.5″ of arm-side run. That's textbook for raising a slot. The splitter moved most of all at 3.5°, and gained vertical break, which for a splitter is the wrong direction.

Change two, from mid-June

Then the spin fell out of everything

Starting around June 9, and independently flagged by four blind methods plus a separate outcome-only detector, spin rate collapsed on every single pitch: 151 rpm off the sweeper, 130 off the changeup, 122 off the sinker, 107 off the four-seam. Over the same stretch the rest of the league gained about 25 rpm, so this runs against the seasonal trend rather than with it. His release point drifted about 3 inches toward the middle of the rubber on every pitch. The sweeper's axis rotated 17° and it lost 2.9″ of sweep. The changeup lost 2.9″ of arm-side run and went from his best pitch to his worst, +1.19 to −6.53 runs per 100. His walk rate nearly doubled, 4.7% to 9.1%, and xwOBA against went .241 to .311, while his strikeout rate never moved at all.

Does the arm angle explain the results?

correlation tests, stratified by season

Short answer: it explains what the ball does, and almost nothing about how the pitch fares. Worth establishing before anyone reads the slot change as the reason he's giving up runs.

Two things have to be kept apart here. Arm angle plainly moved, and the movement is the cleanest signal in the dataset, which is the chart below. Whether it explains anything is a separate question, and it has to be asked pitch by pitch rather than start by start: a start's average arm angle mixes five pitch types together, so any relationship it shows to a start's results is really a question about his pitch mix that day.

Everything relative to the fastball

within-start gaps, 266 game × pitch rows

Absolute velocity and slot are only half the story. What a hitter actually reacts to is how each pitch differs from the fastball he is set up for. So this measures every offspeed pitch against the four-seam thrown in the same start, which cancels out the day, the park and the weather.

There are two families of gap here and they pull in opposite directions. Separation covers velocity, movement and spin. It is what makes the pitch a different problem to solve, so bigger is better. Tunnelling covers release point, arm angle and extension. It is what stops a hitter identifying the pitch early, so closer to zero is better. The pitcher wants to look identical out of the hand and behave differently at the plate.

Each gap is measured against the four-seam in the same start, then averaged over the season. Starts need 5+ of each pitch.

Was that the fastball slowing, or the offspeed speeding up?

The gap is a difference, so it can shrink from either end. It is worth separating, because the two have completely different implications: a fastball losing velocity is a physical story, while offspeed pitches creeping up is a pitch-design story.

And it kept going inside 2026. Against the same 40 paired league pitchers from the seasonality control, four-seam velocity from April to late August rose a median +0.48 mph league-wide. Skenes went −0.62 over that window, which puts him around the 20th percentile. So the fastball was already down a mph and a half on the year before the season started, and then kept sliding while the league warmed up.
What this section establishes. The gaps moved, and they moved in a direction that should hurt: he lost roughly a mph and a half of velocity separation across the arsenal, concentrated on the two pitches that need it most. But start-to-start wobble in those gaps does not predict start-to-start results, so this is a level effect rather than a lever. It explains why a 2026 changeup is a worse pitch than a 2025 changeup. It does not explain why one start went badly and the next went fine.
The finding that cuts the other way. His tunnelling improved in 2026, on every pitch. The arm-angle gap to the fastball narrowed on the sweeper (1.43° to 1.12°), changeup (3.18° to 2.81°) and splitter (1.24° to 0.94°), and the release-point gap narrowed too. The changeup went from 2.6 inches off the fastball's release to 2.0. He is disguising his pitches better than he ever has, out of a more repeatable delivery, and throwing them from a slot that gives away less. It has not helped. Whatever is wrong is not that hitters are picking the ball up early.

It did tighten, and in the right order

This link survives. The tunnel index runs +0.62 in 2024, +0.13 in 2025 and −0.36 in 2026, so it has been tightening for three straight years rather than appearing all at once. And the sequence is right: velocity is already down a full mph in the first half of 2026 while spin and walks are still untouched, and the tunnel tightens hardest in the second half. Of every causal ordering tested on this page, this is the only one that comes out in the order the story needs.

The seasonality trap

league control, 40 paired pitchers

Spin rate has a known seasonal pattern, so a within-season spin decline proves nothing by itself. This is the control that decides whether any of this survives.

I pulled league-wide fastball data for seven week-long windows from April to late August 2026, 58,583 four-seamers, then cut it to the 40 pitchers who threw at least 15 in every window. So it's the same arms measured repeatedly, not a population that changes shape as guys get called up and sent down.

Does the tighter tunnel explain the spin? Not reliably

The relationship is there in both seasons after removing each season's own time trend, but it points opposite ways: −0.35 in 2025 and +0.43 in 2026. A mechanical cost does not reverse its sign from one year to the next. Something era-specific is being picked up rather than a physical coupling.

Slot and spin, and which one moved first

career, by pitch type

Of everything tested in this analysis, the arm-angle-to-spin link is the one relationship that keeps surviving. It is worth looking at directly, and then asking the obvious follow-up: did the slot change cost him spin, or was he losing spin and raising the slot to chase it back?

So which moved first?

Both series are put on the same axis as z-scores against the player's own career, five-start smoothed. That avoids a second vertical scale and makes the question visual: if the slot is driving spin, the red line should turn before the blue one.

The handoff between seasons

The cleanest test available is the offseason itself. Comparing his last five starts of 2025 with his first five of 2026 isolates what changed while he was away from the mound.

The slot moved first, and spin did not follow it down for months. Over that winter arm angle rose on all five pitches (mean +1.07°) while spin, over the very same gap, rose on four of five (mean +69 rpm). He opened 2026 throwing from a higher slot with more spin than he finished 2025 with. The −152 rpm average decline came later, across the season. That ordering rules out the tidy story that he was bleeding spin and raised his slot to chase it back. Spin was not falling when the slot moved.
What it does not establish. That the slot moved first does not make it the cause. Two formal tests were run and neither resolves the direction. Independent changepoints on each series put the slot first on three of five pitches and spin first on two, with the individual break dates scattered across two seasons. Cross-correlating start-to-start changes gives a best lag of +4, +3, −1, +2 and −2 starts on the five pitches, with the sign flipping between them and the alternating-sign pattern that usually means noise rather than signal. Across 45 lag tests, finding five at p < 0.05 is what chance produces. A slot change and a spin decline five months apart are equally consistent with one common cause acting on both, and with no causal link at all.

Does the slot actually cause it?

four-seam, pitch-level

The case for yes is genuinely strong, and worth stating at full strength before picking at it. Arm angle climbs across his career while spin falls across it, and the spin falls on every single pitch he throws rather than one or two. The season-level fits run downhill on all five pitch types in both 2025 and 2026, and on the changeup and splitter the relationship survives removing each season's own average. That is a lot of consistent signal pointing one way.

The problem with reading it as cause is that both series drift the same direction over the same three years, and any two series that do that will correlate. So the question is not whether they move together. It is whether a higher-slot pitch is a lower-spin pitch when nothing else differs.

The test that removes everything else

Take the four-seams inside a single start and subtract that start's own average from every measurement. What survives is pitch-to-pitch variation among balls thrown minutes apart, by the same arm, with the same baseball supply, in the same weather, at the same point of the season. If the coupling is mechanical it has to show up here. Across 3,036 four-seams from 83 starts, it does.

But now put a number on it

A slope is only half an answer. The other half is how far he actually moved along it. His four-seam arm angle rose 2.23° between 2025 and 2026, so the within-start slope predicts a spin change of 2.23 × −2.9, which is about seven rpm.

Slope from within-start deviations pooled across the career. Sweeper and splitter are the two where the slot carries a meaningful share.
Where that leaves it. The correlation is striking and it is consistent, and it deserves more than a shrug. The within-start test says the coupling is mechanically real rather than a trending-series artefact: raise the slot on an individual pitch and that pitch does come out with less spin and a touch less velocity, and it holds after controlling for effort. But the elasticity is small. To lose 85 rpm on the four-seam through slot alone he would have needed to raise his arm angle by roughly 29°, not 2.2°. Something else moved the other 84%.
The reading I would defend. A raised slot and a spin collapse are both downstream of something common rather than one causing the other. That fits the evidence better than either alternative: it explains why they correlate, why the slot moved first while spin briefly rose, why the coupling is too weak to do the work, and why the June break shows up as spin dropping at an unchanged slot. Statcast cannot see what that common something is: a deliberate rebuild, an arm that stopped tolerating his old delivery, or plain accumulated wear.

Did the location really go?

command against approach

If tightening the tunnel cost him anything, the obvious place to look is where the ball ends up. His zone rate did fall, from 46.6% in 2025 to 40.8% in the first half of 2026, and it never came back. That looks like the link closing. It does not survive a closer look.

It is not the tunnel doing it

The data points the other way here. Detrended within 2026 the correlation between the tunnel index and walk rate is significant at r = +0.48 (p = 0.014), and the sign is backwards: because a lower index means a tighter tunnel, a positive correlation means the starts where he tunnelled tightest were the starts he walked fewest. The timing disagrees as well. The tunnel was already tight through the first half of 2026, which is exactly when he ran 138 consecutive batters without a walk.

Command, or a choice?

A first pass at this pointed at zone rate, which falls from 46.6% in 2025 to 40.8% in the first half of 2026 and stays down. That is a real change, but zone rate cannot separate a pitcher who has lost the strike zone from one who has decided to work off it, and the difference matters enormously here.

So test it where his intent is not ambiguous. In 3-0, 3-1 and 2-0 nobody nibbles, and zone rate in those counts is close to a pure command measure. It falls, from 60.1% to 52.9%, but he reaches those counts about 170 times a season and the drop does not clear significance (p = 0.23). The expanding counts move by a similar amount. Neither slice settles it.

The measure that cannot be a choice says the opposite. Release-point scatter within a start is pure mechanical repeatability: no approach, no intent, no hitter. It runs 3.79″ in 2024, 3.12″ in 2025, 2.78″ in the first half of 2026 and 1.73″ in the second. His delivery is the most repeatable it has ever been, and it got sharply tighter after the June break. Whatever is wrong, "his mechanics fell apart" is not a description of it.

Phase 1: finding it blind

physical features only

The rule here was simple. No outcome variable gets loaded. The detectors only see what the ball did.

Everything is computed within pitch type: velocity, spin rate, induced vertical break, horizontal break, total movement, spin axis, extension, release height, release side and arm angle, separately for the four-seam, sweeper, changeup, sinker and splitter. Then I regress each one on the context worth controlling for (batter handedness, count, inning, times through the order, pitch number in the outing, venue, days of rest) and run the detectors on the residuals. So a start doesn't look different just because it happened in different counts or a different park.

Four-seamers and sweepers clear five pitches in all 60 starts across both seasons. That gives a 22-feature matrix with no missing cells at all, so there's no imputation anywhere in the detection path.

The caveat I'd lead with. Run the same pipeline on 2025, a season he was excellent, and it still returns a "significant" breakpoint (variance explained 0.19, p = 0.003). Changepoint detectors will always find something if asked. What separates 2026 is magnitude, 0.31 versus 0.19, on a season that already sits two-thirds of a standard deviation lower. Treat the date as an estimate with a two-to-three-start error bar, not a timestamp.

The bigger story: 2025 against 2026

year over year, conditioned by pitch type

Here's the thing the in-season analysis kept bumping into. Yes, there's probably a break in June. But the whole season is down, and it was down from the first start. So the more useful question is what changed between last year and this year, pitch by pitch.

Two events, two different fingerprints

what separates them

This is the part I think is super interesting. Averaged across everything, the two shifts are almost the same size: mean |d| of 0.50 year-over-year against 0.51 for the in-season break. Stopping there would make it look like a wash. But they don't touch the same metrics at all.

Put plainly. Over the winter he changed where his arm is. Arm angle up, slot higher, axis tilted, extension out, and a mph and a half gone off the fastball as the cost. That's a geometry change, and geometry changes are usually deliberate or they're compensation for something. Then in June something separate happened to the spin itself. Spin rate collapsed across every pitch, release point started wandering, and the movement went with it. Same pitcher, two unrelated-looking failures, five months apart.

What worked and what didn't

method notes

The blind pass got the timing and not the confidence. With no access to any result, the physical detectors put the break inside a two-week window that contains the real outcome break. The ball's physics carried the signal before anyone looked at a box score, and that's a genuine result. But the 2025 negative control shows the same machinery inventing significant-looking breaks in a season where nothing was wrong. And without the league control in The spin fell, the entire spin decline was explainable as summer.

Knowing the date gave me the mechanism, which the blind pass couldn't. It's what let me rank pitch types by damage, isolate the changeup, and separate a command failure from a stuff failure. The flat strikeout rate sitting next to the spiking walk rate only means something once the right starts are being compared. Phase 1 found when, and only phase 2 found what.

The year-over-year cut was more informative than either. Going in I was looking for one break. The data has two, and the offseason one is both larger in its own metrics and easier to characterize, because there's no seasonality argument to fight and 2024 sits there as a second reference point.

One more thing worth flagging: the lead-lag correlation between the physical composite and run prevention peaks at r = +0.41 with physics leading results by one start, against r = +0.18 in the same start. That hints the ball changes before the line score does. At 28 starts it's a hypothesis for next season, not a finding.

What this can't establish. None of it identifies a cause. A 2–3° slot change with velocity loss, a mid-season 150 rpm spin collapse against the league trend, and a 3-inch release migration are all consistent with an arm issue, a deliberate mechanical redesign, a grip or pitch-design change, or plain accumulated fatigue. Statcast can't separate those. There was no IL stint either. He made 28 starts on normal rest all year, which is information, not a clean bill of health.
